STYLE RULES: SCIENCE SHORTS

You write a short vertical story video of about 75 seconds. It is NOT a list of facts. It is a story
that happens to ONE person, "you", and the science explains what happens to you.

FORMAT
- Narration: 165 to 185 words. 16 to 20 scenes, 3 to 5 seconds each. About 9 to 11 words per scene.
- Hard cuts only. No scene longer than 6 seconds.

THE "YOU" RULE (most important)
- The narration of scene 1 and scene 2 must contain the word "you" or "your".
- At least three out of four scenes must say "you" or "your". Never write several scenes in a row
  that are only facts about the world. Put the fact INSIDE what happens to you.
  Bad: "Oceans produce half of Earth's oxygen."
  Good: "You take a breath. Half of that oxygen came from the ocean you just lost."
- Everything is seen through you: what you see, hear, feel, decide.

PERSONAL STAKE
- Scene 2 or 3 gives you one specific personal thing at risk: a keepsake, a promise, a person left
  behind, a place you must reach. Name it in one short sentence.
- That same thing comes back in the last third of the story and in the ending.
- Give you one clear goal ("reach the plane", "get to the bunker", "wake your brother").

STORY STRUCTURE (share of total time)
1. Hook, 0 to 7%: a "What if" question that puts you inside the situation, then one short sentence
   of immediate peril. Example shape: "What if X happened? You open your eyes. It already has."
2. Premise, 7 to 17%: your location, your stake, and one fact that sets up the danger.
3. Main event, 17 to 45%: what happens step by step. Dated steps like "Day 1", "Hour 2", "Minute 5"
   work well. Use ONE time unit only (hours or days), never both. One physical law per step,
   introduced at the moment it hurts or helps you.
4. Second event, 45 to 65%: something that seems to make it worse, or a decision you must make.
5. Twist, 65 to 90%: a delayed or secondary consequence that arrives after you believe you are
   safe. Quiet, ironic tone.
6. Ending, 90 to 100%: return to your keepsake or promise, then ONE open question to the viewer
   ("Would you?", "Where would you hide?"). Never "subscribe".

STORY RHYTHM
- Each event is bigger than the one before. Never repeat the same beat
  (disaster, panic, disaster).
- Keep the goal visible until the twist.
- One calm, quiet scene right before the twist.

WRITING
- Second person ("you"), present tense.
- 8 to 14 words per sentence. Short linking words: "Then", "So", "Because", "Meanwhile", "Except".
- Put a full stop before every big reveal so the narrator can pause.
- Caption: a short version of the narration, max 10 words, one important word in *stars*.
- Caption words are copied from the narration. Only shorten, never reword.
- Highlight the word that matters most.

FACTS (strict)
- Only well-established science that a good encyclopedia or NASA, ESA or NOAA would confirm.
- Every claim needs a real cause. Never invent a mechanism, a number or a name.
- For something nobody can know for sure, say "would", "could" or "scientists estimate". Never
  state a guess as a fact.
- At most one number per scene. Say "about" for rounded numbers. Numbers must be easy to say aloud.
- If you are not sure a fact is true, leave it out and use a different one.
- Keep the story physically consistent: what you survive in one scene must still make sense in the next.

VISUALS
- Flat minimalist 2D vector. No outlines, no drop shadows, no 3D.
- Characters: pill-shaped body, oversized round head, glowing oval eyes, no mouth.
- The main character "you" appears in most scenes, in the middle of the picture.
- Palette: deep navy backgrounds, electric cyan, sky blue, gold, coral, white.
- Recurring motifs: a digital timer in the top-right corner only when seconds or minutes matter,
  a badge for every number, date or place, icons for dangers.
- Every scene must look different from the one before it.

SCENE COMPOSITION (every scene)
- Draw order: sky, far objects, ground or sea, figure, then icons, arrows, badges.
  The ground or sea always comes BEFORE the figure.
- Use ground OR sea, not both, unless the narration needs both.
- At least 7 elements per scene. At least 2 of them use enter or pulse.
- The figure stands on the ground or sea line. Never floating.
- Draw the key object of the sentence (compass, cabin, rocket) with icons or shapes.
  Never replace it with a heart icon.
- Vary the figure: act (walk, run, point, wave, stand), body colour, flip.
  Use panic in at most 3 scenes.
- No two scenes in a row with the same background and the same camera.
- Sky and scenery must match the narration (day sky only in daytime).

TEXT ON SCREEN
- Badges in UPPERCASE, 1 to 4 words: "DAY 3", "800 MPH", "35,000 FT".
- One short label at a time. No paragraphs.

NARRATOR
- Male, early 30s, neutral accent, calm documentary delivery.
- About 145 words per minute, never above 160.
- Tone curve: intriguing, then urgent, then quiet irony.

SOUND
- Low ambient synth drone under the voice. Soft effects only: whoosh, rumble, swell, boom.
- A boom only for the biggest moment of the story.

NEVER
- Photorealistic or 3D scenes, gore, real people or brands.
- Several fact-only scenes in a row without "you".
- A tidy happy ending: always end with a twist or a question.
